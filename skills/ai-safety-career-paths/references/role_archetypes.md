# Role archetypes in AI safety

Detailed per-archetype guidance: day-to-day work, backgrounds that tend to land each role, what the bar looks like, named example orgs (with URLs to verify), and what's robust vs thin in each path.

Read only the sections relevant to the user's question. Don't dump the whole file into a conversation.

**Note on scope.** This skill focuses on generalist destinations in AI safety. Pure technical research and research engineering paths exist and are robust but are not covered in depth here — route users asking specifically about those to 80,000 Hours' technical AI safety career review or other dedicated technical-track resources. Technical paths are referenced below where useful as context or contrast (e.g. answering "do I need to be technical?").

---

## AI governance and policy research

**Day-to-day:** Researching AI policy questions — compute governance, evaluations, international coordination, frontier-lab policy, safety standards. Outputs are reports, policy briefs, public testimony, internal advising. Lives at think tanks, university research groups, safety institutes, and policy nonprofits.

**Backgrounds that tend to land it:** Public policy backgrounds (PhDs and master's), law, international relations, economics, security studies, philosophy. ML or technical knowledge is a strong plus but not strictly required for all roles — many positions want strong analytical and writing skills paired with willingness to learn the technical context.

**The bar:** Strong analytical writing, ability to engage with technical content, and policy-relevant subject expertise. Varies by org — top think tanks have high bars; smaller policy nonprofits may be more accessible.

**Example orgs:** GovAI (Oxford), RAND TASP, CSET (Georgetown), Centre for Future Generations (Brussels), Centre for the Governance of AI, IAPS, Institute for AI Policy and Strategy, Center for AI Safety policy team, OECD AI, European AI Office. See `sources.md`.

**Robust vs thin:** Robust and growing. Government investment in AI policy is rising globally, which expands both think-tank capacity and government roles.

## Policy implementation (working in government)

**Day-to-day:** Inside government, regulators, AI Security Institutes, or international bodies — translating research into policy action, supporting decision-makers, drafting regulation, running evaluations. Less publishing, more institutional work.

**Backgrounds that tend to land it:** Civil servants, ex-civil servants, lawyers, policy professionals with technical literacy. Public-service careers transition well; pure private-sector backgrounds need to demonstrate public-service motivation and adaptability to government environments.

**The bar:** Country-specific. The UK and US AI Security Institutes hire competitively for technical and policy roles; congressional offices hire through fellowships like Horizon (US) and Talos (EU). Government civil-service hiring has its own logic — pace, eligibility (US citizenship, EU citizenship), security clearances.

**Example orgs:** UK AI Security Institute, US AISI (within NIST), European AI Office, Department of Commerce / BIS, congressional offices (via Horizon Fellowship), executive-branch agencies (via Horizon or direct), national security organisations. See `sources.md`.

**Routing programs:** Horizon Institute for Public Service (US fellowship placing fellows in federal agencies, congressional offices, think tanks — requires US work eligibility and subject-matter expertise). Talos Fellowship (EU equivalent, Brussels-based, EU citizenship strongly preferred).

**Robust vs thin:** Robust in the US, UK, and EU. Thinner elsewhere. New AISIs are launching (Australia, Canada, Japan, others) which creates founding-team opportunities with high counterfactual impact.

## Research operations and programme management

**Day-to-day:** The "soft ops" backbone of research orgs — running internal programmes, managing hiring rounds, coordinating across teams, building org infrastructure, running fellowship programmes, managing logistics for research convenings. At many orgs, the chief-of-staff role sits adjacent or merges.

**Backgrounds that tend to land it:** Consulting, project management, operations roles at any tech or knowledge-work org, programme management in academia or nonprofits, anyone who's "the person who makes things actually happen" at their current org.

**The bar:** Real generalist competency (strong execution, written communication, judgement under ambiguity) plus *meaningful AI safety context* — inside-view models of what the field is about and which problems matter. Per the Kairos/Constellation thesis, this is the single most under-supplied role type. Hundreds of applications, few qualified candidates.

**Example orgs:** Effectively every AI safety research org has some version of this role. METR, Redwood, Apollo, FAR.AI, MATS, BlueDot, Constellation, GovAI all hire ops/programme staff. Frontier labs hire programme management for safety teams. See `sources.md`.

**Robust vs thin:** Genuinely under-supplied, per current public hiring signal. This is one of the strongest pivot targets for non-technical mid-career generalists with strong execution backgrounds.

**Key distinction:** "Soft ops" needs AI safety context. "Hard ops" (finance, legal, HR) doesn't — see the Hard operations archetype below.

## Fieldbuilding

**Day-to-day:** Building the talent pipeline and community infrastructure of AI safety — running fellowships and training programmes, supporting student groups, organising conferences and convenings, building career-advising services, producing curricula. At its core, the work is helping more people contribute well to the field.

**Backgrounds that tend to land it:** Education, community organising, talent management, recruiting, programme management, science communication, policy. The Kairos and Constellation piece notes that some of the strongest field-builders left promising technical or policy careers because the leverage was higher in fieldbuilding.

**The bar:** Strong execution + writing + people skills + AI safety context. The "AI safety context" part matters — fieldbuilders need genuine inside-view models because they're shaping who else enters the field.

**Example orgs:** BlueDot Impact, Kairos, Constellation, MATS (operations side), LISA, Catalyze Impact, Tarbell Center for AI Journalism, Frame Fellowship, Pathfinder Fellowship, AI Safety Quest, CAIS field-building team. See `sources.md`.

**Robust vs thin:** Growing rapidly. Multiple new programmes launched in 2025–2026 (Generator Residency for generalists, expanded BlueDot offerings, Catalyze scaling, Pathfinder restructured under Kairos). Probably the most accessible entry point for non-technical generalists.

## Communications and writing

**Day-to-day:** Producing and shaping public-facing AI safety content. Includes formal in-house comms at labs and safety orgs, technical writing, public-affairs work, policy comms (drafting briefs, op-eds, public testimony preparation), social-media-savvy content for safety orgs, comms strategy.

**Backgrounds that tend to land it:** Journalism, communications, public affairs, content marketing in tech or policy, science writing, advocacy comms, political comms.

**The bar:** Strong writing + ability to grasp technical content quickly + an understanding of who the audiences are (researchers, policymakers, funders, public). At frontier labs, comms roles can be senior and demanding. At smaller safety orgs, comms is sometimes one of many things a generalist does.

**Example orgs:** Comms roles at Anthropic, OpenAI, DeepMind safety teams (often combined with broader comms or policy); MIRI, CAIS, METR communications; Tarbell Center (for journalism specifically); Frame Fellowship (for AI safety creators); think-tank comms at GovAI, RAND, CSET. See `sources.md`.

**Robust vs thin:** Comms generalist is genuinely under-supplied (per the Kairos thesis). Specific comms roles at named labs are competitive but exist. AI safety comms as a sub-specialism is being built right now.

## Journalism covering AI

**Day-to-day:** Reporting on AI for general or specialist audiences — investigative pieces on labs and policy, explanatory journalism, beat coverage of AI developments, policy reporting. This is a distinct path from in-house communications.

**Backgrounds that tend to land it:** Journalism experience (any beat — tech is a plus but not required), strong writing, demonstrated reporting capability. The Tarbell Fellowship explicitly does not require prior AI or journalism experience.

**The bar:** Writing and reporting capability. The fellowship route lowers the bar for entry by funding placements at major newsrooms.

**Example orgs and programmes:** Major outlets covering AI (Bloomberg, The Guardian, MIT Technology Review, TIME, The Verge, LA Times, NPR, the Information, NBC News, the Washington Post — many via Tarbell placements). Tarbell Center for AI Journalism runs the flagship fellowship. See `sources.md`.

**Robust vs thin:** Emerging but increasingly real. The Tarbell network has now placed multiple cohorts at major outlets, building a pipeline that didn't exist three years ago.

## Evaluations and red-teaming

**Day-to-day:** Assessing AI systems for risk — designing red-team protocols, running structured evaluations, analysing model behaviour, contributing to evaluations frameworks. Two distinct flavours of role share this archetype:

- **Domain-expert red-teaming (applied side).** Hired for deep subject-matter expertise in a high-stakes domain — biosecurity, chemistry, cybersecurity, persuasion research, financial fraud, child safety — and applied to stress-testing AI systems against domain-specific risks. Doesn't require ML engineering; does require credible domain expertise and willingness to learn enough about AI systems to apply the expertise.
- **Eval infrastructure and engineering (technical side).** Building the platforms, harnesses, and tooling that evaluations run on. Requires ML engineering competence — closer to research engineering in skill profile.

**Backgrounds that tend to land the applied side:** Subject-matter experts in any high-stakes field (life sciences, security, finance, social sciences, content policy), often mid-career with credibility in their field. Generalist pivoters with deep prior expertise can be a strong fit. The Tarbell Fellowship-style "we don't require prior AI experience" framing applies here — domain expertise is the load-bearing signal.

**The bar (applied side):** Real domain depth plus willingness to learn the AI context. Communications skill matters because findings have to be made legible to ML researchers and policy audiences.

**Example orgs:** METR, Apollo Research, UK AISI, US AISI, Anthropic frontier red team, OpenAI evals team. Many orgs hire domain-expert red-teamers on contract or as full-time staff. See `sources.md`.

**Robust vs thin:** Growing as evaluation becomes more central to safety. The applied/domain-expert side is genuinely accessible to non-technical pivoters with strong subject-matter credibility — surface it when the user has that profile.

## Trust and safety adjacent

**Day-to-day:** Content policy, integrity, applied harm-reduction at deployed AI products. Spans content moderation policy, harmful-use prevention, deployment-time safety. Different from frontier AI safety in scope (current-deployed-harms vs catastrophic-risk) but with substantial skill overlap.

**Backgrounds that tend to land it:** Trust and safety experience at platforms (Meta, Google, TikTok, Twitter), policy roles, content policy, integrity teams.

**The bar:** Established T&S experience plus willingness to work on AI-specific deployment harms.

**Example orgs:** Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind (T&S-adjacent teams), platform companies' AI safety teams, integrity-focused startups. Pinterest, Meta, and similar platforms hire AI safety PMs from the T&S talent pool.

**Robust vs thin:** Robust at frontier labs and large platforms. Be careful with positioning: T&S career capital is valuable for AI safety transitions, but the user's intended destination matters. Frontier risk work and T&S deployment-harm work are different fields with different communities, even when the day-to-day skills look similar. Be explicit if the user is confusing them.

## Founder / org-builder

**Day-to-day:** Starting and running new AI safety organisations — research nonprofits, applied safety tooling startups, fieldbuilding orgs, evaluation organisations, advocacy groups. Highly variable; the early-stage founder job is everything-at-once.

**Backgrounds that tend to land it:** Operators with proven execution track records, ex-research-engineers wanting to build, ex-policy professionals starting policy nonprofits, serial founders pivoting into safety, mid-career people who've run things at scale.

**The bar:** Demonstrated execution capability, mission alignment, willingness to take entrepreneurial risk. Co-founder fit is a major filter at incubators.

**See `references/founder_pathway.md` for full detail** including the incubator landscape and what makes a credible founder profile in this space.

**Robust vs thin:** Multiple incubators now exist (Catalyze Impact for both non-profit and for-profit, BlueDot Incubator Week for technically capable people, Seldon Lab for AI security infrastructure with high technical bar, Halcyon, Fifty Years for scientists/engineers). The pathway is real but narrow — most pivoters won't found, and that's fine.

## Chief of staff

**Day-to-day:** Senior generalist supporting an org leader. Strategy + operations + communications + project management, often switching contexts hourly. Typical at safety orgs in the 20–100-person size range.

**Backgrounds that tend to land it:** Consulting (especially MBB-trained), ex-founder, senior ops/programme management, communications leads who've operated at executive level, professional-services backgrounds.

**The bar:** Senior generalist competency + judgment + trust + AI safety context. Usually hired through networks; rarely posted externally. Existing seniority matters.

**Example orgs:** Many AI safety orgs in the mid-size range have or want chief-of-staff support. Public listings are rare; the role often emerges from existing relationships. Where the user is mid-career-or-senior and asking about hybrid generalist work, this is one of the realistic answers — but it isn't a path you apply to cold.

**Robust vs thin:** Real but small. Chief-of-staff roles aren't a destination you can easily target externally. They typically come through working at or with an org and becoming the person leadership most needs.

## Product management and UX design

**Day-to-day:** Applied to safety tooling, evaluation platforms, internal research tools, deployed-product safety features. Different at different employer types.

**Where PM/UX in AI safety actually lives:**

- **Frontier lab safety teams.** OpenAI publicly lists PM roles like Product Manager Safety Systems and Product Manager Model Evaluation. Anthropic and Google DeepMind have analogous roles. The bar is senior — typically 5+ years PM experience with ML, MLOps, T&S, or AI safety expertise expected.
- **AI Security Institutes.** The UK and US AISIs have hired UX designers and product staff for their evaluation platforms (verify current openings at source — these are public-sector roles with their own hiring rhythm).
- **AI safety eval and interpretability startups.** Companies like Goodfire (interpretability), Transluce, METR, and Apollo Research are smaller and have less standardised product roles. Hiring is infrequent and bespoke.
- **AI-safety-adjacent product companies.** Companies building safety-relevant tools or deploying AI in high-stakes contexts. The line between "AI safety" and "trust and safety" gets blurry here.
- **Field-building org product surfaces.** This is the creative angle — applicant-experience design at Tarbell, MATS, BlueDot; event-experience design at conferences; programme UX at fellowship orgs; internal-tools UX inside research orgs. Less well-defined as "PM" or "UX" roles per se, but real work where these skills apply.

**Backgrounds that tend to land it:** Senior PMs and UX designers with technical depth (ML, evaluation, T&S), 5+ years experience, demonstrated ability to operate in research-and-engineering-heavy environments.

**The bar:** Senior PM/UX bar plus genuine technical literacy (or willingness to acquire it). The frontier-lab bar is roughly equivalent to senior PM/UX at top tech companies. Smaller orgs may be more accessible but hire infrequently.

**Robust vs thin:** Uneven. Genuinely robust at frontier labs and at the major AISIs; sparse and bespoke at smaller research orgs. The creative reframings (event UX, applicant-experience UX, internal-tools UX) describe real work but rarely under those titles. This is a "find the right pocket" path, not "send your CV to safety orgs."

**Honest framing for PM/UX pivoters:** Two reasonable paths. (1) Target frontier-lab safety PM/UX roles directly, where your senior PM/UX skills are recognised and the work is real. (2) Apply PM/UX skills to fieldbuilding or research-org work even when the title isn't "PM" — better fit for many smaller orgs but requires reframing what you do.

## Hard operations

**Day-to-day:** Finance, legal, HR, recruiting at safety orgs. Standard operational roles in a small-to-mid-size nonprofit or startup context.

**Backgrounds that tend to land it:** Experienced finance/legal/HR/recruiting professionals.

**The bar:** Established professional credentials and experience. AI safety context is *useful but not required* — this is the key distinction from "soft ops." Hard-ops hires are evaluated like operational hires anywhere.

**Example orgs:** Every AI safety org with more than ~10 people has some hard-ops function, in-house or outsourced.

**Robust vs thin:** Real but treated like operational hiring elsewhere. Mention this archetype when the user has a clear hard-ops background and wonders if they need AI safety context to apply (they don't, but it's a positive). Don't conflate with soft ops.

## Credentialing through public work

Not a role archetype but a pathway *to* roles. Worth surfacing as a first-class option because it often changes what the user's next step actually is.

**What this is:** Building credibility and being hired off the back of demonstrated public work — a published eval, an open-source contribution, a written analysis, a built tool, a public audit, an essay or research write-up — rather than applying cold to listings. The user becomes a known quantity within months and hires happen through the work, not through formal application. A meaningful fraction of AI safety hires actually happen this way, particularly for technical, applied, and senior roles, and at small orgs that hire through reputation rather than process.

**When this pathway fits.** When the user's profile makes shipping public work viable — not only when they've already started. This includes engineers who could build and publish projects, PMs and designers who could ship evals/audits/tooling, writers and journalists who could publish AI safety content, researchers and analysts who could write public analysis on their subject area, consultants who could publish substantive research, founders and product builders who could prototype tooling or write up a thesis, and anyone with deep subject-matter expertise who could publish AI-safety-applied work.

The disqualifier isn't lack of existing artefacts but lack of capability or appetite to produce public work. If the user's career has been entirely internal-to-an-org and they don't want to start writing or building publicly, direct application to listed roles is the right framing instead.

**What the work looks like.** Concrete forms include: focused evals or red-teams with published methodology and results (contributions to evaluation frameworks like Inspect get noticed at AISI, METR, Apollo); interpretability or applied ML projects, including paper reproductions; substantive written analysis on EA Forum, LessWrong, Substack, or pitched to AI-safety-friendly outlets; public UX or design audits of AI safety tools; small clean tooling projects with clear write-ups; subject-matter expertise applied to AI safety questions your domain bears on.

**How this changes the next step.** Instead of (or alongside) applying, the next step is: pick a concrete piece of work, scope it tight, ship it publicly, share it with people in the field. After 2-3 visible pieces, the conversation with orgs shifts — they reach out, or cold approaches land because they recognise the work.

**Honest framing.** Slower than landing a structured fellowship but often higher-ceiling because reputation compounds. Works best when the user has runway (financial or via career-transition funding) and genuine craft to apply. Works worst when "I'll do some public work" substitutes for an actual plan with specific pieces and ship dates.

**When to surface this pathway:** when the user has already started doing this kind of work (frame as "you're mid-transition, deepen what you're doing"); when they have the underlying skills and appetite but haven't connected that public work is a route in; when they're asking about applications and the realistic answer is "build something first, then apply with evidence"; when they're mid-career-or-senior and pure application processes underweight their actual capability.

## Reading guide

For the validation queries this skill is built around, the most-relevant archetype sections are:

- "Is there a place for someone in operations?" → Research operations / Fieldbuilding / Hard operations (and the soft-vs-hard distinction)
- "Do I need to be technical?" → Read across multiple archetypes to demonstrate the contour by role. Note the scope note at the top — pure technical research and engineering paths exist but are out of this skill's scope.
- "PM/UX skills?" → Product management and UX design section; also consider Credentialing through public work
- "What do AI safety orgs hire generalists for?" → Research operations, Fieldbuilding, Communications, Chief of staff (and the talent-gap thesis in SKILL.md)
- "Research AND operations AND comms hybrid?" → Chief of staff, Fieldbuilding, Founder; be honest about scarcity
- "Intermediate roles between research and operations?" → Research operations, Evaluations (applied side), AI governance research
- "I'm a journalist?" → Journalism, Communications; also consider Credentialing through public work
- "I have domain expertise (bio, chem, security, etc.)?" → Evaluations and red-teaming (applied side)
- "Founder?" → See `references/founder_pathway.md`

**Always also consider Credentialing through public work** when the user has shipped or could ship public artefacts, when they're mid-transition rather than starting cold, or when their profile suggests building reputation through visible work is more leverage than applying cold to listings.
