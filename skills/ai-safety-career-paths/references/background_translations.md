# Background-to-role translations

Per-background guidance: which role archetypes typically fit, what to lead with, what's a stretch, where the user is likely to be over- or under-confident about their fit.

Read the section matching the user's background. If their background is hybrid (e.g. consultant who's also founded), read multiple. If their background isn't here, work from the closest match plus the heuristics at the end.

---

## Operations / project management / programme management

**Strong fits:** Research operations and programme management; Fieldbuilding; Chief of staff (if senior enough); Hard operations (if their ops experience is finance/HR-flavoured).

**What transfers:** Execution under ambiguity, running programmes from scratch, coordinating across functions, judgment about priorities, written communication, hiring and people management, vendor management, budget management.

**What to lead with on applications:** Specific things made or shipped. "Ran X programme for Y people," "scaled Z process from N to M," "managed budget of W." AI safety orgs are small and need people who'll just do the thing. Concrete delivery beats generic ops framing.

**What's underweighted on a typical CV:** Anything they've built from scratch. "Started X" or "set up Y" matters more than incumbent execution. Pivoters often underweight the founder-shaped parts of their ops work.

**What's overweighted:** Generic "operations excellence" framing, certifications (PMP etc.), formal process methodology. AI safety orgs care less about Lean Six Sigma than about whether you'd actually run the thing well.

**The gap to close:** AI safety context. Per Kairos/Constellation, soft-ops roles need real inside-view models of the field. Read the field, talk to people, attend events, write reflections. The course route (BlueDot) is one option; sustained engagement is another. Don't apply cold without context.

**Common misframing:** "I'll just do ops at any safety org." Better framing: target specific role types (programme management at a fellowship org; research ops at a small research lab; chief-of-staff to a leader you can credibly support) where the match is concrete.

**Honest framing if it's not a great fit:** If the user's ops experience is purely operational backend (e.g. supply chain, manufacturing ops, infrastructure ops) without people-and-programmes work, the path is harder. Redirect them toward hard-ops roles or sectors where their specific ops type maps (e.g. compute infrastructure has its own niche).

## Journalism / writing / reporting

**Strong fits:** Journalism covering AI; Communications and writing (especially at safety orgs or think tanks); Tarbell Fellowship as a structured entry route.

**What transfers:** Translating complex technical content for a non-specialist audience; reporting under deadline; building source networks; ability to push back on PR-shaped narratives; investigative judgment; storytelling craft.

**What to lead with:** Published work — clips, samples, anything with a byline that shows reporting depth. For AI-adjacent work, even better. Outlet brand carries weight but is not necessary.

**What's overweighted:** Generic "communications professional" framing if the user has actual reporting experience. Journalism is its own craft and the AI safety ecosystem distinguishes journalists from in-house communicators.

**The gap to close:** AI technical literacy, AI policy literacy, and the specific landscape of the safety community. Reading widely (BlueDot's intro course, Zvi Mowshowitz's Substack, the Alignment Newsletter, AI policy primers from think tanks) closes most of it. The Tarbell training programme closes the rest formally.

**Two routes:**
1. **Tarbell Fellowship route** — apply for placement at a major newsroom as an AI reporter. Application competitive; placement at outlets including Bloomberg, the Guardian, MIT Tech Review, TIME, the Verge. Most legible entry point for an early-or-mid-career journalist.
2. **In-house comms route** — pivot from journalism into communications work at a safety org or think tank. Different career; the safety community values journalism-trained writers for explanation and outward-facing comms.

**Honest framing:** Pure print/print-adjacent journalism in this space is more vibrant than three years ago but still relatively small. Be honest that the field is being built rather than mature. The Tarbell network is now multiple cohorts deep, which makes it less speculative than it was.

## Product management

**Strong fits (in descending order of accessibility):** Trust and safety adjacent PM at platforms doing AI work; Product manager at frontier-lab safety teams (senior bar); Product manager at AI safety eval/interpretability startups; Chief-of-staff-flavoured generalist work where PM skills apply; Fieldbuilding or research org ops with PM craft applied differently.

**What transfers:** Working in research-and-engineering-driven environments; defining and prioritising under ambiguity; translating between research and product; running cross-functional execution; user research methods; metrics design; the discipline of shipping.

**What to lead with:** Specific products built, problems solved, and metrics moved. For roles in safety, work that touches AI, evaluation, T&S, or any product where reliability/safety/trust was a load-bearing constraint maps better than generic consumer PM.

**What's overweighted:** Generic "PM at a tech company" framing. The frontier-lab safety PM bar is senior and specific; it isn't just "you've been a PM."

**The gap to close:** AI safety context, ML literacy depth (for technical PM roles), and specific exposure to evaluation, T&S, or model-eval workflows. The route in depends on the destination — see the Product management section in role_archetypes.md.

**Creative angles to mention:** Many PM skills apply at fieldbuilding orgs (cohort programme PM, applicant experience design, internal-tools PM at research orgs) under different titles. If the user is open to non-"PM" titles that use PM craft, this expands the option set substantially. Be honest that the role won't always be called PM and the comp structure will look different from a frontier-lab PM role.

**Honest framing if it's a stretch:** If the user is early-career PM without ML or T&S exposure, frontier-lab safety PM roles are a hard target. Better: build relevant context first (work on an AI product team somewhere; do BlueDot; contribute to an AI-adjacent open-source eval project) before targeting safety-specific PM roles.

## UX / design

**Strong fits:** UX design at frontier-lab safety teams; UX design at AI Security Institutes (evaluation platforms); UX at safety eval/interpretability startups; applicant-experience and event-experience design at fieldbuilding orgs.

**What transfers:** Designing interfaces for complex, ambiguous tasks; user research methods (which translate to stakeholder research in policy contexts); ability to make abstract or technical work accessible; iteration discipline; prototyping under uncertainty.

**What to lead with:** Specific design work shipped. Especially work on technical/researcher-facing products, complex data interfaces, evaluation tooling, or anything where the user was an expert audience.

**The gap to close:** AI safety context plus comfort embedded in research-and-engineering-driven teams. Many AI safety orgs don't have established design culture, so the role often involves educating colleagues about design as much as doing design.

**Two routes:**
1. Frontier-lab and AISI roles where UX design has a clear, named home. Senior bar but the role is real.
2. Apply UX craft at fieldbuilding orgs and research orgs where the title won't be "UX Designer" but the work is real (cohort experience design, application-process redesign, internal-tools redesign, conference/event experience).

**Honest framing:** UX in AI safety is genuinely thinner than UX at established product companies. The role count is small. The path requires patience and willingness to take roles where the title undersells the work, or to target the limited number of established roles at frontier labs and AISIs.

## Law / policy

**Strong fits:** AI governance and policy research; Policy implementation (in government, at AISIs, in regulatory bodies); think-tank work; international AI policy. For mid-career lawyers, possibly chief-of-staff or general-counsel-style roles at safety orgs.

**What transfers:** Analytical writing; familiarity with regulatory frameworks; ability to work in formal institutions; comfort with ambiguous policy questions; international institutional knowledge; ability to advise decision-makers.

**What to lead with:** Specific policy or legal work done. Areas of technical-or-regulatory expertise (export controls, antitrust, IP, international agreements, AI Act implementation). Public-service motivation if pivoting toward government.

**The gap to close:** AI technical literacy and AI safety community familiarity. The technical depth needed depends on the role — pure policy roles need less than technical-AI-policy roles. Established law-and-policy professionals can typically close this gap quickly by sustained reading and engagement.

**Routing programmes:** Horizon Institute (US fellowship, places lawyers at congressional offices, federal agencies, think tanks). Talos Fellowship (EU equivalent). These are the most legible entry points for policy-and-law pivoters in the US and EU.

**Honest framing:** This is one of the more robust pivot paths. Demand for AI-and-law expertise is rising at think tanks, government, and AI orgs. Don't undersell the user's legibility — law-and-policy backgrounds are well-recognised by safety orgs.

## Biotech / hard science / non-ML technical

**Strong fits:** Evaluations and red-teaming (if subject-matter expertise is relevant — biology, chemistry, cyber); Information security (if cybersecurity-adjacent); AI governance research (if the user has policy interest); Founder pathway (technical-founder route).

**What transfers:** Technical literacy, ability to grasp ML concepts, rigorous experimental thinking, willingness to engage with complex systems, established research credibility.

**What to lead with:** Specific scientific work and demonstrated ability to learn new technical domains. For evaluation roles, subject-matter expertise in any high-stakes domain (bio, chem, cyber, persuasion) is genuinely useful.

**The gap to close:** ML literacy specifically. For research engineering roles, this requires real ML retooling (months to a year). For domain-expert red-teaming or governance research, less ML is needed; AI context and policy literacy are more load-bearing.

**Two routes:**
1. Retool into ML for research engineering or technical safety research. Coefficient Giving's CDTF explicitly funds this kind of transition.
2. Apply subject-matter expertise to evaluation, governance, or applied work without becoming an ML researcher. This is often the higher-leverage path for established scientists.

**Honest framing:** Pure-science backgrounds without policy interest and without willingness to retool are a harder fit. The user's openness to policy work and to learning ML matters a lot. Ask about both before recommending.

## Consulting / strategy

**Strong fits:** Research operations and programme management; Chief of staff (if senior); Fieldbuilding leadership; Founder pathway. Soft-ops in general is well-suited to consulting backgrounds.

**What transfers:** Structured thinking, written communication, ability to ramp on new domains quickly, slide-craft, stakeholder management, project execution under pressure, analytical rigour.

**What to lead with:** Specific projects delivered. Concrete outcomes, not just engagement names. Anything entrepreneurial — internal initiatives founded, businesses started inside the firm, programmes built — translates particularly well.

**What's overweighted:** Brand of firm. Less load-bearing than the user thinks. Safety orgs care more about what was actually done.

**The gap to close:** AI safety context plus willingness to take smaller orgs and lower comp than consulting. The cultural shift from consulting to safety nonprofit work is non-trivial — pace, autonomy, lack of formal frameworks, smaller teams.

**Honest framing:** Strong fit on paper for soft-ops, programme management, and chief-of-staff roles. Reality check: the user needs to actually want a small-team execution role, not "consulting-but-in-AI-safety." If they want analytical impact-focused work, AI policy research at think tanks may be a better fit than ops.

## Founder / entrepreneur

**Strong fits:** Founder pathway (see `references/founder_pathway.md`); Chief of staff at safety orgs; senior generalist roles at fieldbuilding orgs.

**What transfers:** End-to-end ownership; ability to operate without formal structure; comfort with risk; demonstrated execution; ability to recruit and manage; resourcefulness; willingness to do whatever the org needs.

**What to lead with:** What was built, raised, shipped, scaled. Outcomes over titles.

**The gap to close:** AI safety context, mission alignment depth, technical understanding of why the problem matters. For founders specifically, having a credible thesis on what to build matters more than founding pedigree.

**Two routes:**
1. **Found in AI safety.** Read `references/founder_pathway.md`. Pre-idea entrepreneurs can apply to incubators like Catalyze Impact (generalist-and-technical-friendly), BlueDot Incubator Week, Halcyon. Seldon Lab has a high technical bar — not the right fit for non-technical founders.
2. **Join as senior operator / chief-of-staff.** Established founders often have more leverage joining a growing safety org as senior operator than starting one from scratch. The trade-off is autonomy vs. counterfactual context (joining gives more context, founding gives more autonomy).

**Honest framing:** Founding in AI safety is real but narrow. Most pivoters won't and shouldn't. The right test is whether the user has a specific thing they want to build, not whether they want to be a founder.

## Software engineering (non-ML, looking at generalist pivot)

**Note:** This skill focuses on generalist pivots. If the user is a software engineer asking about retooling toward ML to pursue technical research or research engineering roles, route them to 80,000 Hours' technical AI safety career review and dedicated technical-track resources rather than answering inside this skill.

For software engineers exploring **generalist** pivots into AI safety:

**Strong fits:** Research operations and programme management; Fieldbuilding; Trust and safety adjacent. Domain-expert evaluations work if they have specific subject-matter expertise (e.g. security background → AI security evaluations). Founder pathway for those wanting to build product.

**What transfers:** Systems thinking, technical literacy, ability to ship, comfort with engineering culture, ability to work credibly with engineering-heavy teams. Many soft-ops roles benefit from someone who can both run programmes and credibly engage with research teams.

**The gap to close:** AI safety context and genuine openness to non-engineering work. Some engineers discover mid-pivot that they actually want technical work — better to surface this honestly upfront. If they want generalist work, the gap is mostly AI safety domain context (via BlueDot or sustained engagement) and willingness to take roles where coding isn't the day job.

**Honest framing:** Software engineering backgrounds are valuable in AI safety because the field is engineering-heavy, but the value cashes out differently for generalist vs technical roles. For generalist work, your engineering background gives you credibility with research teams but doesn't substitute for AI safety context. Worth asking the user directly whether they want to keep engineering as their day job (in which case route to technical paths outside this skill) or move into generalist execution work (in which case this skill applies).

## Education / academia (non-ML)

**Strong fits:** Fieldbuilding (especially programme design and curriculum work); AI governance research (if academic discipline is policy-relevant); Communications and writing (if the user has science writing experience).

**What transfers:** Curriculum design, programme management, mentorship, research literacy, structured thinking, written communication, ability to work in mission-driven institutions.

**What to lead with:** Concrete teaching, programme, or research outcomes. Anything that scaled (number of students reached, programmes launched).

**The gap to close:** Pace and operational tempo of nonprofit / startup work, which is different from academic time. AI safety context.

**Honest framing:** Fieldbuilding is a particularly good match. The user should also consider whether their academic identity is essential to their happiness — if so, an academic AI safety role (CHAI, GovAI, university-affiliated centres) may be better than a nonprofit role.

## Communications / marketing / public affairs (outside journalism)

**Strong fits:** Communications and writing at safety orgs; Public affairs at safety institutes; Fieldbuilding (programme comms); Frame Fellowship (for AI safety creators specifically).

**What transfers:** Audience modelling, messaging discipline, content production, channel strategy, ability to translate complex content for specific audiences.

**The gap to close:** AI technical literacy enough to write credibly; AI safety community familiarity; for some roles, journalism credibility (which marketing/PR backgrounds don't automatically have).

**Honest framing:** Comms in AI safety is its own discipline. Marketing/PR backgrounds transfer best when the user is willing to learn deep technical and policy content. Pure marketing backgrounds without analytical orientation may struggle. Frame Fellowship is a structured route for content creators specifically.

## Finance / banking

**Strong fits:** Hard operations roles at safety orgs (finance, fund administration); grantmaking roles at funders; Founder pathway (especially for-profit founder route); operations roles at funder organisations.

**What transfers:** Quantitative rigour, analytical discipline, comfort with risk modelling, financial operations, regulatory navigation.

**The gap to close:** Mission alignment depth — finance pivoters often have to demonstrate they want this work, not just an exit from finance. AI safety context.

**Honest framing:** This is a real path but narrower than ops or fieldbuilding. The user should consider grantmaking (Coefficient Giving and others hire programme officers), funder operations, or financial roles at safety orgs. For founders, finance backgrounds plus a technical co-founder is a common founder shape.

## Heuristics for backgrounds not covered above

When the user's background isn't explicitly here, work from the closest analogy plus these heuristics:

- **What did they actually do, not what is their job title?** Operations, founding, comms, analysis, engineering — these underlying capabilities are what safety orgs hire for. Job titles often obscure them.
- **Where have they demonstrated specifically valuable capability?** Concrete delivery in any context translates.
- **How willing are they to retool?** This determines whether technical paths are accessible.
- **What's their geographic and financial situation?** Most safety jobs are in SF, London, DC, Brussels with some remote. Career-transition funding (Coefficient CDTF, BlueDot) can address financial constraints.
- **What do they actually want?** Some pivoters want close-to-research work. Others want execution. Others want public-facing work. The right match depends on this more than on background.
