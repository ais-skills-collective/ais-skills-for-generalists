# AIS Perspectives Map

A map of the major intellectual camps in AI safety, used by the `ai-safety-101` skill
on contested and evaluative questions. See `../SKILL.md`.

## What this file is — and is not

This is a **coverage aid**, not a citation allowlist. Its job is to make sure that on
a contested question you represent the real spread of views — that you can name the
camps, state each one's strongest case, and attribute it to real advocates — rather
than defaulting to a single framing.

It is deliberately **open and illustrative**. The camps and names below are
representative, not exhaustive, and not a closed list of who may be cited. When you
cite a *specific factual claim* from any source here, that claim still goes through
the normal rules in `../SKILL.md` and `source-registry.md` — this file does not make
anything quotable-as-fact.

Two cautions:

- **No false balance.** The camps differ in empirical support, institutional
  backing, and how many researchers hold them. Represent the real distribution of
  expert opinion, attribute positions to named advocates, and do not manufacture a
  fake 50/50.
- **Camps are not boxes.** Many researchers hold views across more than one camp —
  caring about both near-term harms and long-term risk, for example. Treat these as
  positions, not tribes, and say so when a question cuts across them.

## The camps

### 1. Catastrophic / existential-risk-focused alignment
- Core claim: sufficiently advanced AI could pose risks up to and including human
  extinction or permanent loss of human control; aligning AI goals with human intent
  is the central problem and is unsolved.
- Representative voices: MIRI (note: pivoted around 2024 from technical alignment
  research toward policy advocacy for halting frontier AI development — distinguish
  older technical work from current output; see source-registry.md); much of the
  Alignment Forum community; frontier-lab safety teams; the Center for
  Human-Compatible AI (CHAI, led by Stuart Russell); researchers such as Yoshua
  Bengio (who shifted toward this position around 2023); the Centre for the
  Governance of AI (GovAI) for the policy-research version.
- Use it for: the strongest version of the "urgent catastrophic risk" position and
  the technical-alignment framing of the problem.

### 2. Near-term harms / AI-ethics critique
- Core claim: the urgent harms are present and structural — bias, surveillance,
  labour effects, environmental cost, concentration of power — and the
  existential-risk framing diverts attention, talent, and money away from them, and
  at times serves the interests of the labs.
- Representative voices: the Distributed AI Research Institute (DAIR, Timnit Gebru);
  the AI Now Institute; the ACM FAccT research community; researchers such as Emily
  Bender; the Ada Lovelace Institute for the policy version.
- Use it for: the strongest version of the critique *of* the x-risk framing and the
  present-day-harms agenda. Do not reduce this camp to "they don't take AI
  seriously" — its claim is about which harms are urgent and who benefits from the
  framing.

### 3. Empirical / evaluation-first
- Core claim: claims about AI risk should be grounded in measurement of what models
  actually do, not in theory or speculation — build evaluations and test.
- Representative voices: METR; Apollo Research; the national AI Safety/Security
  Institutes; Epoch AI for quantitative trends.
- Use it for: what the current evidence shows, and as a methodological check on both
  speculative-risk and dismissive claims. This is more a stance on method than an
  ideology.

### 4. Skeptic / risk-overstated, and pro-openness
- Core claim (two related strands): (a) catastrophic and existential framings are
  speculative and overstated — current systems are tools, not agents, and
  over-regulation is the larger danger; (b) open model weights make AI safer through
  scrutiny and by preventing a few actors concentrating control, so "safety" should
  not become a pretext for closed, concentrated AI.
- Representative voices: this camp is the least institutionally consolidated —
  attribute to named individuals rather than implying a canon. Examples include Yann
  LeCun (a prominent public skeptic of the x-risk framing) and open-weights
  advocates; parts of the academic ML community treat x-risk as speculative.
- Use it for: the strongest version of the "overstated" and "openness as a safety
  strategy" positions. Be honest that it has thinner curated sourcing than camps
  1–3 — that is a fact about the source landscape, not a reason to omit the view.

### 5. Governance / policy (spans the political spectrum)
- This is a domain, not a single ideology, and the policy world is not monolithic.
  Think tanks span hawkish and dovish, left and right, and disagree on regulation,
  compute controls, and international coordination.
- Representative voices: the Center for Security and Emerging Technology (CSET);
  RAND; the Center for a New American Security (CNAS); Brookings; GovAI.
- Use it for: the range of policy positions. Do not present "the policy view" as one
  thing — name which institution and which stance.
