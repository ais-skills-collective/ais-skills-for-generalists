# Funder archetypes

Detailed reference for the funder archetypes named in the main skill. Read the relevant sections when the user's project shape and stage are known.

For each archetype: what they fund, ask range, format, geographic notes, robustness, fit signals, named examples. Specific cohort timings, application windows, and current focus areas live at the funder URLs in `sources.md`. Don't commit to those specifics from this file.

At the end: a short "What funders read for" section for pitch-review prompts.

---

## Speculative and regranting platforms

Fast-cycle funders for individuals, small teams, and discrete projects. Lowered friction at the small-to-medium end of the funding stack since 2024.

**What they fund:** Discrete projects, public artefacts (write-ups, evals, prototypes), upskilling stints, conference travel, experimental work, individual researcher stipends. Generally not multi-year org runway.

**Ask range:** Low thousands to mid-five-figures typically; some platforms go higher for selected projects. Manifund grants are commonly in the $1k–$25k range. LTFF makes grants to individuals and small teams typically in the tens of thousands to low six figures.

**Format and cadence:** Rolling applications. Manifund is a marketplace — applicants post proposals publicly, regrantors decide. SFF Speculation Grants are awarded automatically to most applicants of the main SFF process. LTFF reviews on a regular cadence with public decisions.

**Geographic notes:** Generally global. Some restrictions on adversarial nations and on ability to receive funds in certain jurisdictions. Verify per platform.

**Robustness:** Well-established and growing. Manifund has become a significant entry point for new AI safety projects since 2024. LTFF has been the established individual-grant funder for years.

**Fit signals:** Small or discrete deliverable, public-facing output, builds credibility for larger applications later, founder is early in their AIS track record, project would benefit from public visibility (Manifund specifically).

**Fit anti-signals:** Multi-year team runway, large compute spend, projects that benefit from confidentiality, ask too small to be worth the funder's review overhead (under ~$500–$1k), ask too large to credibly review on a fast cycle.

**Named examples:**
- **Manifund** — public marketplace for AI safety and EA-aligned projects, regrantor-driven
- **SFF Speculation Grants** — pre-screening grants in the SFF main process, awarded to most applicants
- **Long-Term Future Fund (LTFF)** — established individual and small-team grant fund for longtermist work, including AI safety
- **Cosmos Institute / Cosmos Grants** — $1-10k fast grants for projects at the intersection of AI and human flourishing; truth-seeking, autonomy, and decentralisation focus; 90-day prototype expectation
- **Emergent Ventures (Mercatus Center)** — fast grants for "zero to one" ideas including AI safety; non-AIS-specific but accepts AIS projects
- **Institute for Progress (IFP) Launch Sequence** — $10k honorarium plus funder matchmaking for short AI safety pitches (alignment, control, evals, AI security)
- **AE Studio Research** — open grant programme for human-agency-aligned AI projects
- **EA Infrastructure Fund** — adjacent platform, funds EA-ecosystem infrastructure projects including AIS-adjacent ones
- **AI Alignment Foundation (AIAF)** — grants and fiscal sponsorship for independent researchers in neglected alignment approaches; relevant for unaffiliated researchers who need a fiscal sponsor

## Strategic safety-aligned grantmakers

The largest pots in the AI safety funding landscape. Mostly fund organisations and major projects. Most operate proactively (they reach out to grantees) rather than via open RFPs, with periodic exceptions.

**What they fund:** Operating budgets of nonprofit research orgs, fieldbuilding orgs, advocacy and policy orgs, large research programmes, major fellowship and incubation infrastructure. Sometimes individual researchers or smaller projects via specific tracks.

**Ask range:** Low six figures for smaller commitments; multi-million for major grants. Coefficient Giving is regularly the largest single grantor in the space.

**Format and cadence:** Mixed. Coefficient Giving is mostly proactive with occasional open RFPs for specific focus areas. SFF runs an "S-process" algorithmic allocation across rounds. Longview Philanthropy advises and deploys for major donors. Schmidt Sciences has periodic open RFPs (Science of Trustworthy AI, others). AISTOF and AI Safety Fund (Frontier Model Forum) operate with their own cadences.

**Geographic notes:** Most are global. Some exclude adversarial nations. Schmidt Sciences explicitly encourages cross-geographic collaborations. AI Safety Fund is supported by the major frontier labs.

**Robustness:** Well-established and substantial. This is where the largest concentration of capital sits.

**Fit signals:** Established org or strong founder credibility, specific thesis aligned with the funder's stated focus areas, existing network in the field, ability to absorb and deploy six-or-seven-figure funding, capacity to report and demonstrate impact.

**Fit anti-signals:** Cold applicant without prior AIS engagement, ask much smaller than the funder's typical grant size, generic mission without sharp thesis, applied tooling that fits VC better than grant funding.

**Named examples:**
- **Coefficient Giving** — Open Philanthropy rebrand; largest funder in the existential risk and AI safety space
- **Survival and Flourishing Fund (SFF)** — second-largest AI safety funder; uses the S-process for allocation; open globally; Speculation Grants as entry-level path
- **Longview Philanthropy** — advises and deploys for major donors across longtermist causes including AI safety
- **Future of Life Institute (FLI)** — programmes and grants across AI safety, biosecurity, nuclear, longtermism
- **Foresight Institute** — AI for Science & Safety Nodes programme and other RFPs; rolling applications
- **Schmidt Sciences** — Science of Trustworthy AI Research, AI Interpretability RFPs, AI2050 fellowship; periodic open RFPs; academic-leaning
- **AI Safety Tactical Opportunities Fund (AISTOF)** — focused tactical funding for AI safety opportunities
- **AI Safety Fund (Frontier Model Forum)** — labs-supported fund for AI safety research grants
- **Astera Foundation** — private foundation funding AI safety; also invests in for-profits to redeploy capital to AIS
- **Astralis Foundation** — $25M annual giving for transformative AI work
- **Founders Pledge** — directs entrepreneur charitable giving across cause areas including AI safety
- **Cooperative AI Foundation (CAIF)** — funds research into cooperative intelligence of advanced AI
- **Center on Long-Term Risk (CLR) Fund** — funds work on worst-case suffering risks from AI
- **AI Risk Mitigation (ARM) Fund** — grants for technical research, policy, and training programmes
- **EA Infrastructure Fund** — adjacent funder for EA-ecosystem infrastructure including AIS
- **Laidir Foundation** — AI governance: policymaker education, policy design, civil society coordination

**Topic-focused grant programmes within this archetype:** Several strategic grantmakers run programmes restricted to specific output types — AI journalism (Tarbell Center, Omidyar Network Tech Journalism Fund, FLI Digital Media Accelerator), advocacy and awareness (Nonlinear AI Safety Advocacy Grants), policy research (CSET Foundational Research Grants), specific technical areas (Schmidt Sciences Interpretability RFP). When the user's project shape matches one of these tracks, surface them specifically.

**Long-tail private foundations:** Several smaller or proactive foundations operate by invitation or via referral rather than open application: Good Ventures (Moskovitz/Tuna), The Navigation Fund (Jed McCaleb), GTR Foundation (Berlin-based, Jan Beckers), The Dreamery Foundation (Steven Schuurman), Saving Humanity from Homo Sapiens (SHfHS), Meta Charity Funders. Mention these when the user has the network density or org credibility to be a plausible target.

## Career-transition funds

Funds the *person*, not the organisation, during a focused work period or career pivot. The AI Safety Career Paths skill handles upstream career framing; this skill handles eligibility and process detail.

**What they fund:** Living stipends during pivots, focused work periods on AI safety, upskilling and self-study, professional training, transition and exploration time, unpaid internships, small project costs adjacent to a transition.

**Ask range:** Living-stipend-shaped — typically several thousand to mid-five-figures, with some funds going higher for longer commitments.

**Format and cadence:** Rolling for most. Coefficient CDTF accepts applications globally on a rolling basis. BlueDot's Career Transition Grant runs for full-time AI safety work. BlueDot Rapid Grants ($50–$10k range) are for specific projects, not living stipends.

**Geographic notes:** Coefficient CDTF is worldwide. BlueDot funds are global but UK-anchored organisation; verify any specific restrictions.

**Robustness:** Well-established. These funds explicitly exist to support the pivot bottleneck.

**Fit signals:** Pivoter with credible plan, existing AIS engagement or strong adjacent background, financial constraint is the binding factor, focused work period (not open-ended), specific deliverables or learning goals.

**Fit anti-signals:** Org runway needs (use grant funders or VC instead), purely exploratory without a plan, replacement for general employment income beyond a defined transition window.

**Named examples:**
- **Coefficient Giving Career Development and Transition Funding (CDTF)** — rolling, global, broad scope
- **BlueDot Career Transition Grant** — for people working full-time on AI safety, includes mentorship
- **BlueDot Rapid Grants** — $50–$10k for specific projects, not living stipends

For deeper pivot framing — whether the user should transition, which track, what role archetype to target — route to the AI Safety Career Paths skill.

## Incubator-attached funding

Funding bundled with structured programme participation. The programme is part of the value; funding is one component alongside mentorship, co-founder matching, and network access.

**What they fund:** Pre-idea or idea-stage founder teams, seed funding for selected startups out of cohorts, programme stipends, sometimes follow-on investment from associated funds.

**Ask range:** Highly variable. Programme stipends are typically several thousand to low five figures. Seed investments out of incubator-associated funds can run from low six figures to seven figures depending on the incubator.

**Format and cadence:** Cohort-based, with application windows. Catalyze runs cohorts approximately annually. BlueDot Incubator Week is short and intensive. Seldon Lab and others run their own batches.

**Geographic notes:** Variable. Catalyze runs remotely with some in-person elements. BlueDot Incubator Week is London-based. Seldon Lab is SF-based. Fifty Years and Constellation operate from specific hubs. Verify per programme.

**Robustness:** Catalyze is well-established with ~15 incubated orgs. Seldon has a short but credible track record (alumni acquired by xAI and Anthropic). Others are younger.

**Fit signals:** Pre-idea or idea-stage, value the programme structure and co-founder matching as much as the money, willing to commit to a multi-week or multi-month programme, fit the specific incubator's thesis.

**Fit anti-signals:** Already raising follow-on (apply direct to VC instead), already operating with team and traction (most incubators want earlier-stage), thesis doesn't match the incubator's focus (e.g. non-technical pivoter applying to Seldon).

**Named examples:**
- **Catalyze Impact** — non-profit and for-profit AI safety org incubator; generalist and technical friendly
- **BlueDot Incubator Week** — short intensive London programme with funding for selected teams
- **Halcyon Futures** — entrepreneurial nonprofit + VC fund for AI safety, security, assurance
- **Fifty Years** — scientists and engineers building startups in AI safety and adjacent areas
- **Constellation Incubator** — pre-idea entrepreneurs and early-stage orgs
- **Seldon Lab** — AI security infrastructure accelerator, technical-only

For founder framing upstream of funding choice, route to the AI Safety Career Paths skill's `founder_pathway.md`.

## Safety-aligned VC and angel networks

For-profit funding for applied safety tooling startups. Has expanded materially since 2024 as a category.

**What they fund:** Equity investment in for-profit and PBC safety tooling companies — evaluation infrastructure, interpretability tooling, agent monitoring, hardware security, content provenance, AI safety for enterprise, red-teaming products.

**Ask range:** Pre-seed to Series A typically; sizes follow standard VC conventions for each stage. Angel checks at the smaller end; institutional VC at the larger end.

**Format and cadence:** Cold and warm intro; rolling. Warm intros materially improve odds across this category. Most operate with traditional VC partner-meeting cadences rather than open RFPs.

**Geographic notes:** Most are US-based but invest globally. Some have geographic preferences; verify per fund.

**Robustness:** A meaningful number of named funds now exist. Some are dedicated AI safety focused; others are generalist VCs with significant safety investments. The category is younger than grant funding but materially expanded since 2024.

**Fit signals:** For-profit or PBC structure, applied tooling with commercial potential, technical co-founder credibility, identifiable customer or market signal, willingness to take on VC-shaped expectations (growth, equity dilution, exit pressure).

**Fit anti-signals:** Pure research orientation, nonprofit mission, no commercial path, no technical depth on the founding team, mission-alignment-only without business model. These should go to grant funders or incubators instead.

**Named examples:**
- **Lionheart Ventures** — AI safety focused
- **Juniper Ventures** — AI safety focused
- **Mythos Ventures** — AI safety focused
- **Anthology Fund** — Anthropic-backed
- **Polaris Ventures** — AI safety focused
- **Safe AI Fund** — AI safety focused
- **Macroscopic Ventures** — Swiss VC focused on reducing suffering risks from AI; hybrid model with both grants to nonprofits and investments in for-profits
- **Metaplanet** — Jaan Tallinn's evergreen VC; profits redeployed to AI safety nonprofits

Generalist VCs also invest in safety-aligned startups but aren't included here as their focus is broader; if the user is targeting generalist VC, treat this skill as out of scope and recommend they use general startup advisory resources.

## Government and quasi-government

Public-sector and quasi-public funding for AI safety research. Materially increased since 2024 as governments stand up AI safety institutes and dedicated programmes.

**What they fund:** Research grants, fellowships placing researchers inside government, applied AI safety R&D, dual-use research, small business innovation for AI safety tooling.

**Ask range:** Wide. Fellowship stipends are living-stipend shaped. Research grants range from tens of thousands (small programme grants) to multi-million (SBIR Strategic Breakthrough Awards reach up to $30M for late-stage commercialisation; major NSF AI safety grants can be substantial).

**Format and cadence:** Formal RFPs with defined application windows. Government cycles are typically 3–6 months from submission to award. Fellowships run in cohorts.

**Geographic notes:** Strongly geography-locked, with exceptions. US programmes (NSF, SBIR/STTR, Horizon) generally require US work eligibility. UK ARIA and UK AISI programmes are UK-anchored but some accept international applicants (AISI Alignment Project accepted from 42 countries). EU programmes are EU-citizenship-leaning. Verify per programme.

**Robustness:** Well-established for legacy programmes (NSF, SBIR); newer for AI-safety-specific programmes. The trajectory is expanding.

**Fit signals:** Academic affiliation or registered legal entity, willingness to handle formal RFP application cycles, US/UK/EU residency or work eligibility for geography-locked programmes, research topic matching the programme's stated focus.

**Fit anti-signals:** Individual independent researcher without affiliation for most programmes, fast cycle needs (government is slow), small ask sizes below typical programme minimums, geography mismatch.

**Named examples:**
- **UK AISI Alignment Project** — UK-funded; recent cohort drew applicants from 42 countries
- **UK ARIA** — UK Advanced Research and Invention Agency
- **US NSF AI safety programmes** — multiple programme areas
- **SBIR/STTR via NIST** — US small business innovation grants; AI Agent Standards Initiative track recently established
- **Anthropic Fellows** — operates in US, UK, Canada; weekly stipend plus compute credits
- **OpenAI Safety Fellowship** — open to applicants from CS, cybersecurity, social sciences, privacy, HCI
- **Horizon Institute for Public Service** — US placements in federal agencies, congressional offices, think tanks; requires US work eligibility
- **Talos Fellowship** — EU policy fellowship, Brussels-based, strong EU citizenship preference
- **AI2050 (Schmidt Sciences)** — fellowship programme for exceptional people working on AI for societal benefit
- **CSET Foundational Research Grants** — supports research on national-security implications of AI (Georgetown)
- **Vista Institute for AI Policy** — fellowship sponsoring students and recent graduates in AI policy research
- **Economics of AI Fellowship (Stripe)** — fellowship for graduate students and early-career researchers in foundational economics of AI

Fellowships overlap with the AI Safety Career Paths skill's role-archetype framing; this skill covers funding mechanics, AI Safety Career Paths covers fit-to-role.

## Individual donors and HNW networks

Major individual donors and donor-advised funds. Often accessed via warm intro and ongoing relationship rather than application.

**What they fund:** Wide range, often gap-filling or bridge funding, sometimes follow-on after a credible grant round. Mission-aligned giving across nonprofit and increasingly impact-aligned for-profit.

**Ask range:** Wide; varies by donor. Often in the range of tens of thousands to mid-six-figures for individual donors, with major HNW donors making larger commitments.

**Format and cadence:** Mostly relationship-driven and warm-intro. No formal application process for many. Donor circles and donor-advised platforms (some active, some discontinued — check current status) act as semi-formal intermediaries.

**Geographic notes:** Variable; depends on donor and on the recipient's ability to receive funds tax-efficiently.

**Robustness:** Hard to characterise as a category because of variability. Established as a meaningful slice of total AIS funding but harder to systematically engage with.

**Fit signals:** Existing relationship in the AIS community, project that benefits from a champion rather than a process, willing to invest time in donor cultivation, fundraiser or org leader with credibility.

**Fit anti-signals:** Cold applicant with no network, project that needs predictable cadence rather than relationship cultivation, small ask sizes where the relationship overhead doesn't justify the donor's attention.

**Named examples:** This category is harder to enumerate publicly. Donor-advised platforms have come and gone (AISafety.com's funding page documents some discontinued ones). Major donor introductions typically happen through Coefficient Giving, Longview, BlueDot, Constellation, founder networks, EAG events, and similar. Route the user to community channels rather than naming individuals.

## Prize, RFP, and bounty-based

Bounded competitions and project-specific bounties. A smaller slice of the funding landscape but useful for specific use cases.

**What they fund:** Specific outputs against a stated brief — research papers on a defined topic, written analysis, working prototypes, evaluation suites, prizes for solving specific problems.

**Ask range:** Variable. Bounties are usually small to medium. Major prizes can run substantially higher.

**Format and cadence:** Submission deadlines and judging cycles. Some platforms run continuously with bounties posted against specific projects.

**Geographic notes:** Generally global. Some restrictions on adversarial nations or on payment routing.

**Robustness:** A real but smaller category. FLI's worldbuilding contest and similar competitions have run multiple iterations. Decentralised AI safety bounty platforms exist but the category is younger.

**Fit signals:** Specific output the user already wants to produce, fit with a posted bounty or RFP, willing to compete on quality, doesn't need ongoing funding.

**Fit anti-signals:** Need for sustained runway, project doesn't match any posted brief, project benefits from confidentiality.

**Named examples:**
- **FLI grants and contests** — periodic programmes including worldbuilding-shaped contests
- **Superlinear Prizes** — decentralised bounty platform offering prize money for delivering on specific AI safety projects
- **Periodic lab RFPs** — frontier labs occasionally post RFPs for safety research; verify at lab careers and research pages

## What funders read for

Across funder archetypes, a small set of signals carries weight in pitch and application review. Specific funders weight these differently — a Coefficient Giving review prioritises different things than a Manifund regrantor's quick read or a safety VC partner meeting. Treat these as common ground, not universal standards.

When reviewing a user's pitch or proposal draft, prompt against:

- **Specific thesis.** Not "we want to do AI safety work" but "we believe X is a missing piece of work, here's why no one else is doing it well, here's what we'd build." Funders read for specificity over breadth.
- **Theory of change articulated end-to-end.** How does the proposed work lead to safer AI? What does success look like at each step? Funders are sceptical of theories of change with hand-waving in the middle.
- **Neglectedness.** Why is this work not already happening? If it is happening elsewhere, why is this approach distinctive?
- **Founder or team credibility.** What has the team built, run, or shipped before? Real AIS engagement (courses, public artefacts, prior work, network in the field) is a distinct signal funders look for, beyond generic execution credibility.
- **Mission alignment with the funder's stated focus.** Is the work actually within the funder's focus areas? Pitches that obviously haven't read the funder's RFP or stated thesis are an immediate filter.
- **Ask realism vs scope.** Does the requested amount match the proposed scope? Asks that are too small for the work to succeed, or too large for the scope, are red flags.
- **Evidence of existing engagement with the field.** Public artefacts, prior funding, network density, courses completed, conversations with people in safety. Filters out applicants who arrived cold.

**Pitch review approach:** Give substantive feedback against these dimensions. Be explicit that this is a general framework — the user should tighten the pitch for the specific funder they're targeting. **Always recommend human review** before submission. Specific human review channels are listed in `sources.md`.
